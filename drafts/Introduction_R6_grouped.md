**Mandarin-Speaking Children's Interpretation and Expression of Actions and Results: Evidence from Resultative Verb Compounds and Monomorphemic V1s**

# 1. Introduction

Describing an event involves identifying what someone did and whether that action brought about a particular result. Someone can squeeze a lychee without getting it open, or wipe a mirror without getting it clean. To talk about such events, children must learn what different expressions commit a speaker to with respect to the result, and which information to express when they describe an event themselves.

Mandarin resultative verb compounds (RVCs) and their monomorphemic first verbs make this learning problem especially visible. An RVC combines two verbal components: the first (V1) expresses an action, and the second (V2) expresses the resulting state of the affected entity (Li & Thompson, 1981; Sybesma, 1999). *Tā bāo-kāi-le lìzhī* "She peel-open-PERF lychee" thus states both that she peeled the lychee and that the lychee came open. The V1 alone, as in *Tā bāo-le lìzhī* "She peeled the lychee," expresses the action but does not itself express that resulting state. For many Mandarin monomorphemic verbs, the result is not entailed. Tai (1984) observed that *shā* "kill" can be used of an event in which the victim did not die, whereas the corresponding RVC *shā-sǐ* "kill-dead" cannot, and Talmy (1991, 2000) identified verbs of this kind, whose result is implied but can be cancelled, as a major type in Mandarin. A listener who hears a V1 description may nevertheless infer that the result was attained, that is, that the event *culminated*. Because this culmination inference is not entailed, it can be cancelled by further information (J. Liu et al., 2026).¹ RVCs and their V1s therefore differ in whether the result is entailed or only inferred, and this difference becomes observable when an action is performed but its result is not attained.

Research on acquisition shows that Mandarin-speaking children produce RVCs early, although their command of RVC event structure develops gradually (Chen, 2008; Deng, 2019), and that by around five years of age they treat RVCs as requiring the result in comprehension (J. Liu et al., 2026). Their interpretation of the corresponding V1s is less adult-like. Chen (2017) found that the transparency of RVCs helps children learn the state-change meanings of the compounds, but that children have difficulty unpacking the meanings of the individual verbs, and children have been reported to interpret simple verbs such as *guān* "close" as entailing a change of state that adults do not require (Chen, 2017).⟦待核：Chen 2017 全文中的具体数据与出处（或为 Chen 2005/2008）⟧ J. Liu et al. (2026) similarly found that 5-year-olds, who rejected RVC descriptions of events without the result, also rejected descriptions with monomorphemic causative verbs in such events more often than adults did. This pattern raises the question of how children interpret monomorphemic V1s, and it cannot be answered by contrasting V1s with RVCs alone. Adults' acceptance of result cancellation also varies across monomorphemic verbs, forming a cline rather than a binary contrast (Chen, 2018). Whether a child accepts a V1 description of an unattained result may therefore depend on which V1 is used and on how that action is related to the result in question.

The present study examines Mandarin-speaking 4- and 5-year-olds' interpretation and production of RVCs and monomorphemic V1s, with adults tested as a reference group. It compares RVC and V1 descriptions of events in which a target result is either attained or not, and it compares V1s that share an object and a target result but were selected to differ in how strongly they are associated with that result. To verify and characterize this difference, adults rated each V1–object expression for *result likelihood*, how likely the target result is to follow the action, and *action typicality*, how typical the action is as a means of achieving that result. Section 1.1 reviews what is known about the relation between V1, V2, and the RVC as a whole, and why existing classifications of V1s leave the relation between a V1 and a particular result underspecified. Section 1.2 explains how result likelihood and action typicality characterize the association between a V1 and a result. Sections 1.3 and 1.4 review children's comprehension and production of RVCs and V1s, and Section 1.5 presents the research questions and predictions.

## 1.1 V1, V2, and the Classification of Monomorphemic V1s

Prior work on RVCs bears on three properties of the V1 that are relevant here: which V1s can enter RVCs, what constrains their combination with V2, and what a V1 conveys about a result on its own.

First, the V1 of an RVC is not restricted to transitive activity verbs; Huang (2006), for example, analyzes resultatives with unergative V1s. Membership in a verb class therefore does not by itself determine which compounds exist. The present study is restricted to agentive transitive RVCs in which V2 predicates a resulting state of the object, which keeps the action–result–participant configuration comparable across materials.

Second, V1–V2 combination is constrained by the relation between the two components. Li (1990, 1995) derives the argument structure of V–V compounds from the thematic properties of V1 and V2 and from the interaction of thematic and causative hierarchies. Han (2021) analyzes V1–V2 sentences as describing a force exerted by one entity on another: V1 names the action through which the force is realized, and V2 names the result state of the affected entity. On either account, whether a compound is possible depends on how a particular V1 relates to a particular result. Correspondingly, the same V1 can combine with different V2s and the same V2 with different V1s: *tī* "kick" forms both *tī-dǎo* "kick-fallen" and *tī-kāi* "kick-open," and *miè* "extinguished" follows both *chuī* "blow" and *huī* "wave."

Third, what a V1 conveys about a result on its own has been characterized in ways that do not align. Mandarin monomorphemic verbs such as *shā* "kill" and *guān* "close" have been analyzed as activity verbs that do not encode the result (Tai, 1984), as implied-fulfillment verbs whose result is implicated but cancellable (Talmy, 1991, 2000), and as causative verbs that nevertheless allow zero-change construals (Martin et al., 2023). Kou and Hohenstein (2022) distinguish four fulfillment types, from verbs that leave the result unspecified to verbs that entail it, and propose diagnostics for assigning English and Mandarin verbs to them. Chen (2018), using adult ratings, found that the acceptability of cancelling the result varies along a cline across verbs, and J. Liu et al. (2026) showed that such cancellation also depends on context, such as the presence of *hǎojǐcì* "several times." These accounts differ in their criteria and in where they draw boundaries, but they share one feature: result strength is treated as a property of the verb.

This verb-level characterization is a limitation for studying how a V1 description is interpreted for a particular event. A comprehension task presents a V1 with a particular object and asks about a particular result, and, as the combination facts show, a V1's relation to a result depends on the result in question: kicking is plausibly a reliable way of knocking over a bottle but a less reliable way of opening a door. Work on sentence processing likewise shows that comprehenders' expectations about events depend on the combination of a verb with its arguments rather than on the verb alone (Bicknell et al., 2010; McRae & Matsuki, 2009). Previous child studies have described differences among V1s in terms of the strength of the result inference without measuring the particular action–result relation depicted. The present study does not propose a new classification of V1s. Instead, it characterizes the selected V1–object expressions by their association with a specified target result, measured along two dimensions.

## 1.2 Result Likelihood and Action Typicality

The relation between a V1 action and a target result, which we refer to as *action–result association*, can be evaluated from either end. *Result likelihood* starts from the action: once the V1–object action has been performed, how likely is the target result to follow? *Action typicality* starts from the result: given the target result, how typical is the action as a means of achieving it? Blowing on a candle, for example, is both likely to put it out and a typical way of doing so, whereas waving at it is neither. Both dimensions concern the relation on which RVC formation depends, namely how a particular V1 event relates to a particular result (Section 1.1), and both bear on which stage of an event a V1 description is taken to describe.

Result likelihood bears on the culmination inference. Accounts of monomorphemic verbs hold that a verb can imply a result without entailing it and that the strength of the implication varies, whether across verbs (Chen, 2018) or across fulfillment types that differ in the certainty of the result (Kou & Hohenstein, 2022, 2024), but they do not specify what that strength depends on. The present study takes as a working assumption that one relevant factor is how likely the result is to follow the action: the more likely the result, the stronger the expectation that the event culminated, and the less acceptable a V1 description may seem when the result is absent.

Action typicality bears on what counts as a good instance of the action. Actions are understood as directed toward results, and an expression can describe an action aimed at a result while leaving its attainment open (Talmy, 1991, 2000; Kou & Hohenstein, 2024). The actions that achieve a given result can thus be viewed as a goal-derived category, in which typicality is predicted by how well members serve the goal and how often they are encountered as members (Barsalou, 1985). When an action is a typical means of achieving a result, that result is part of the usual course of the action, and an attempt that fails to produce it departs from that course. Because children are reluctant to extend familiar verbs beyond typical instances of the actions they name (Ma et al., 2021), such a failed attempt may make a V1 description less acceptable.

The two dimensions are expected to go together, because an action that reliably produces a result is often also a common way of producing it, although they need not coincide in every case (Kim & Murphy, 2011). The present study uses them jointly: a V1–object expression has a stronger association with the target result than its partner when adults rate the result as more likely to follow the action and the action as a more typical means of achieving it. The study does not attempt to separate the contributions of the two dimensions. Neither rating measures lexical entailment or children's own knowledge; both are adult reference measures of the selected V1–object–result relations.

## 1.3 Children's Comprehension of RVCs and V1s

Comprehension studies of RVCs and V1s present events with or without the result and ask children to judge perfective descriptions of them. Mandarin-speaking children show early sensitivity to the association between the perfective marker *-le* and telic verbs (Li & Bowerman, 1998). Within this paradigm, J. Liu et al. (2026) found that 3-year-olds accepted non-culminating interpretations of both RVCs and monomorphemic causative verbs, whereas 5-year-olds rejected them for RVCs but rejected them for monomorphemic verbs more often than adults did; the adverbial *hǎojǐcì* "several times" increased acceptance of non-culminating interpretations in all age groups. The 5-year-olds thus distinguished the two forms, yet their judgments of V1 descriptions remained stricter than adults'.

These findings, together with Chen (2017), indicate that by five years of age children appear to distinguish V1s from RVCs, in that they do not require the result for V1 descriptions as consistently as for RVC descriptions, but that their judgments of V1 descriptions remain stricter than adults'. Two questions remain open. First, it is not known whether 4-year-olds, who fall between the age groups tested by J. Liu et al. (2026), already distinguish the two forms. Second, previous studies have not asked whether children's stricter judgments of V1 descriptions depend on how strongly the action is associated with the result. If they do, children should reject V1 descriptions of zero-result events more often when the V1 is strongly associated with the target result than when it is weakly associated with it. Because acceptance is a binary judgment, it can show whether children's interpretation of V1s varies with action–result association, but not whether a rejection reflects an inference that the result occurred, a judgment that a description leaving an expected result unaddressed is inadequate (Katsos & Bishop, 2011; Skordos & Papafragou, 2016), or a judgment that the failed attempt is a poor instance of the action (Section 1.2).

## 1.4 Children's Production of RVCs and V1s

Comprehension shows how children evaluate descriptions supplied to them; production shows which information they choose to express when no description is supplied. Children produce RVCs from early on (Chen, 2008; Deng, 2019), and children between 3 and 5 years can produce RVCs with the infixes *-de-* and *-bu-* when primed, which indicates that they treat V1 and V2 as separable components (Hsu et al., 2019). Production research has largely asked whether and when children form RVCs; it has rarely compared descriptions of matched events in which the same action does or does not attain its result.

This comparison is informative because RVC use and mention of the result do not coincide. When the result is attained, a speaker may describe the event with an RVC, name only the action, name only the result (e.g., *miè le* "went out"), or express the action and the result in separate predicates. When the result is not attained, a speaker may describe only the action or mention the result in order to deny it, as in *méi bāo-kāi* "did not peel open." A child who produces fewer RVCs may therefore leave the result unmentioned or express it in another form, so RVC frequency alone does not show whether children encode the result. Distinguishing these possibilities requires coding whether an RVC is used and, among responses without one, whether the action, the target result, or both are expressed, as well as whether a mentioned result is affirmed or negated. A response that affirms the target result in a zero-result event would indicate that the speaker described an unattained result as attained.

## 1.5 The Present Study

The study comprises a comprehension experiment and an elicited-production experiment with independent samples of 4-year-olds, 5-year-olds, and adults. Both use the same 40 videos: ten pairs of actions, each pair sharing an object and a target result, with each action filmed in a full-result and a zero-result version. Each pair was constructed so that one V1–object expression was expected to be more strongly associated with the target result than the other (e.g., *chuī* "blow" vs. *huī* "wave" a candle, with the target result *miè* "extinguished"). Adult ratings of result likelihood and action typicality were collected to verify and characterize this contrast. In eight pairs, the expected stronger-association expression received reliably higher ratings on both dimensions ⟦待核：确认这 8 对的典型性差异逐对可靠⟧; these pairs, comprising eight stronger-association and eight weaker-association V1s, form the materials for the analyses of Experiment 1. The two remaining pairs, in which the typicality difference was reversed or neither difference was reliable, are excluded from these analyses (Section 3).

**Experiment 1** tests children's interpretation of RVC and V1 descriptions in a yes–no judgment task, in which acceptance serves as the measure of interpretation. It asks two questions.

*RQ1.* Do children interpret V1 and RVC descriptions differently with respect to the target result, as indexed by whether acceptance of the two forms differs between full-result and zero-result events?

*RQ2.* Within pairs matched for object and target result, do children interpret stronger- and weaker-association V1s differently, as indexed by whether acceptance of V1 descriptions differs between the two sets, particularly in zero-result events?

RVC descriptions are expected to be accepted for full-result and rejected for zero-result events, although children may accept some RVC descriptions of zero-result events (J. Liu et al., 2026). V1 descriptions are expected to be accepted less often in zero-result than in full-result events, but this difference should be smaller than for RVCs (J. Liu et al., 2026). In zero-result events, stronger-association V1s are expected to be accepted less often than their weaker-association partners, with a smaller difference in full-result events. Because 4-year-olds fall between the age groups tested by J. Liu et al. (2026), no directional prediction is made about differences between 4- and 5-year-olds.

**Experiment 2** examines how children describe the same events in an open-ended video-description task. It first asks whether realization of the target result affects whether children include an RVC in their descriptions. Within responses that contain only monomorphemic verbs and do not mention other results, it then asks whether children express the action only, the target result only, or both in separate predicates. Whether a mentioned target result is affirmed or negated is coded separately. Full-result events are expected to elicit more RVCs than zero-result events, because an absent result is expressed only if the speaker chooses to deny it. The adult ratings are not used as predictors in Experiment 2: they were collected for specified V1–object expressions, whereas the production task supplies no expression and the videos were not rated on these dimensions.

Because the two experiments use independent samples and different response demands, they provide complementary evidence about how children interpret and express actions and results, but they do not test a within-child relation between comprehension and production, and comparisons across tasks remain descriptive.
