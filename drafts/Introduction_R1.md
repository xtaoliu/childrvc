**Mandarin-Speaking Children's Interpretation and Expression of Actions and Results: Evidence from Resultative Verb Compounds and Monomorphemic V1s**

# 1. Introduction

Describing an event involves identifying what someone did and whether that action brought about a particular result. Someone can squeeze a lychee without getting it open, or wipe a mirror without getting it clean. To talk about such events, children must learn what different expressions commit a speaker to with respect to the result, and which information to express when they describe an event themselves.

Mandarin resultative verb compounds (RVCs) and their monomorphemic first verbs make this learning problem especially visible. An RVC combines two verbal components: the first (V1) expresses an action, and the second (V2) expresses the resulting state of the affected entity (Li & Thompson, 1981; Sybesma, 1999). *Tā bāo-kāi-le lìzhī* "She peel-open-PERF lychee" thus states both that she peeled the lychee and that the lychee came open. The V1 alone, as in *Tā bāo-le lìzhī* "She peeled the lychee," expresses the action but does not itself express that resulting state. For many Mandarin monomorphemic verbs, the result is not part of what the verb asserts: Tai (1984) observed that *shā* "kill" can be followed by a clause denying that the victim died, whereas the corresponding RVC *shā-sǐ* "kill-dead" cannot ⟦待核：Tai 例句⟧. A listener who hears a V1 description may nevertheless infer that the result was attained, that is, that the event *culminated*. Because this culmination inference is not entailed, it can be cancelled by further information (Talmy, 1991, 2000; J. Liu et al., 2026).¹ RVCs and their V1s therefore differ in whether the target result is linguistically entailed or only inferred, and this difference becomes observable when an action is performed but its result is not attained.

Research on acquisition shows that Mandarin-speaking children produce RVCs from early on and represent their internal action–result structure (Chen, 2008; Deng, 2019; Hsu et al., 2019), and that in comprehension they become sensitive to whether the result expressed by V2 has been attained (Chen, 2017; J. Liu et al., 2026) ⟦待核⟧. Their interpretation of the corresponding V1s, however, departs from adults': when an action is performed without attaining its result, children reject V1 descriptions of the event more often than adults do (Chen, 2017; J. Liu et al., 2026) ⟦待核⟧. This pattern raises the question of how children interpret monomorphemic V1s, and it cannot be answered by contrasting V1s with RVCs alone. Adults' result-related interpretations also vary across monomorphemic verbs (Chen, 2018), and in children's judgments some V1s have been rejected for no-result events more often than others (Chen, 2017) ⟦待核⟧. Whether a child accepts a V1 description of an unattained result may therefore depend on which V1 is used and on how that action is related to the result in question.

The present study examines Mandarin-speaking 4- and 5-year-olds' interpretation and production of RVCs and monomorphemic V1s, with adults tested as a reference group. It compares RVC and V1 descriptions of events in which a target result is either attained or not, and it compares V1s that share an object and a target result but were selected to differ in how strongly they are associated with that result. To characterize this difference with explicit measures, adults rated each V1–object expression for *result likelihood*, how likely the target result is to follow the action, and *action typicality*, how typical the action is as a means of achieving that result. Section 1.1 reviews what is known about the relation between V1, V2, and the RVC as a whole and why existing classifications of V1s leave this relation underspecified. Section 1.2 explains why result likelihood and action typicality are used to distinguish V1s. Sections 1.3 and 1.4 review children's comprehension and production of RVCs and V1s, and Section 1.5 presents the research questions and predictions.

¹ The study concerns culmination, whether the result expressed by V2 is attained in the depicted event, rather than telicity, the presence of an inherent endpoint in the event structure of a predicate; a telic predicate may still permit non-culminating uses (J. Liu, 2018). Throughout, the *target result* is the result expressed by V2 in the paired RVC; *target* does not imply that the result is intended or expected. In *full-result* events the target result is visibly attained; in *zero-result* events the action is performed but the target result is not attained.

## 1.1 V1, V2, and the Classification of Monomorphemic V1s

Prior work on RVCs bears on three properties of the V1 that are relevant here: which V1s can enter RVCs, what constrains their combination with V2, and what a V1 conveys about a result on its own.

First, the V1 of an RVC is not restricted to transitive activity verbs. Resultatives with unergative V1s have been discussed (Huang, 2006), and some compounds have change-of-state V1s, such as *dòng-liè* "freeze-cracked" (Tham, 2012) ⟦待核⟧. Membership in a verb class therefore does not by itself determine which compounds exist. The present study is restricted to agentive transitive RVCs in which V2 predicates a resulting state of the object, which keeps the action–result–participant configuration comparable across materials.

Second, V1–V2 combination is constrained by the relation between the two components. Li (1990, 1995) analyzes restrictions on the argument structure of V–V compounds in terms of theta-role identification and of the interaction between thematic and causative hierarchies, and Han (2021) locates the constraint in the causal relation itself: the compound is licensed when the V1 event can be construed as bringing about the result ⟦待核⟧. One consequence is that the same V1 can combine with different V2s, and the same V2 with different V1s: *tī* "kick" can form *tī-dǎo* "kick-fallen" and *tī-kāi* "kick-open," and *miè* "extinguished" can follow both *chuī* "blow" and *huī* "wave." The relation between a V1 and a result is thus specific to the particular action and result involved.

Third, accounts of what a V1 conveys about a result on its own disagree in how they classify Mandarin monomorphemic verbs. Talmy (1991, 2000) distinguishes verbs whose meaning includes an intended result whose attainment is only implied from expressions that assert attainment, and treats Mandarin agentive verbs as typically leaving attainment to a following satellite such as V2 ⟦待核：Talmy 类别名称⟧. Tai (1984) argued that Mandarin lacks monomorphemic accomplishment verbs that entail their result, whereas Kou and Hohenstein (2022) argue that Mandarin includes verbs whose result is entailed ⟦待核⟧. Chen (2018) found graded differences in adults' acceptance of result cancellation across monomorphemic verbs, which she characterizes as a cline of state-change implicature strength ⟦待核⟧, and Martin et al. (2020) and J. Liu et al. (2026) treat the culmination inferences of monomorphemic causatives as cancellable and sensitive to context ⟦待核⟧. These accounts thus use different criteria, assign verbs to categories that do not align, and characterize result strength as a property of the verb.

This verb-level characterization is a limitation for studying how V1 descriptions are interpreted in a particular event. A comprehension task presents a V1 with a particular object and asks about a particular result, and, as the combination facts show, a V1's relation to a result depends on the result in question: *tī* "kick" is plausibly a reliable way of knocking over a bottle but not of opening a door. Work on sentence processing similarly shows that comprehenders' expectations about events depend on the combination of a verb with its arguments rather than on the verb alone (Bicknell et al., 2010; McRae & Matsuki, 2009) ⟦待核⟧. Prior child studies have accordingly attributed differences among V1s to the strength of the result inference or to aspectual class (Chen, 2017, 2018) without measuring the specific action–result relation depicted ⟦待核⟧. The present study does not propose a new classification of V1s. Instead, it characterizes the selected V1–object expressions with respect to their target results along two dimensions, described next.

## 1.2 Result Likelihood and Action Typicality

The relation between a V1 action and a target result can be evaluated from either end. *Result likelihood* starts from the action: once the V1–object action has been performed, how likely is the target result to follow? *Action typicality* starts from the result: given the target result, how typical is the action as a means of achieving it? Both dimensions concern the relation that RVC formation draws on, namely whether a V1 event can be construed as bringing about a particular result (Section 1.1), but they bear on the interpretation of a V1 description in different ways.

Result likelihood corresponds most directly to the strength of the culmination inference discussed above. Accounts of monomorphemic verbs hold that a verb can imply a result without asserting it and that the strength of the implication varies (Chen, 2018; Kou & Hohenstein, 2022), but they do not specify what that strength depends on. One natural candidate is how likely the result is to follow the action: the more likely the result, the stronger the expectation that the event culminated, and the less acceptable a V1 description may seem when the result is absent. This is the dimension most directly tied to the question of how children interpret the event stage that a V1 describes.

Action typicality is related to likelihood but not reducible to it. Actions are understood as directed toward results: an expression can describe an action aimed at a result while leaving its attainment open (Talmy, 1991, 2000; Kou & Hohenstein, 2024). The actions that achieve a given result therefore form a goal-derived category, and typicality in such categories reflects both how well a member serves the goal and how frequently it is encountered as a member (Barsalou, 1985) ⟦待核⟧. The two dimensions are therefore expected to be correlated, because an action that reliably produces a result is often also a common way of producing it. They need not coincide, however, because items that best serve a goal are not always judged the most typical (Kim & Murphy, 2011) ⟦待核⟧. Calling someone, for example, is a typical way of waking them up, whereas pinching is a less typical way that may nonetheless be judged more likely to succeed.

Typicality matters for interpretation for a further reason. Children extend a familiar verb more readily to typical than to atypical instances of the action it names (Ma et al., 2021) ⟦待核⟧. If children's representation of an action includes the result it usually brings about, an attempt that fails to bring about that result may be judged a poor instance of the action, and a V1 description may be rejected for that reason rather than because the child expects the result. Ma et al.'s measure concerned how typical an action is as an instance of a verb, not as a means to a result, so this extension is a hypothesis to be tested. Because the two dimensions may be correlated in any set of materials, an association between children's judgments and likelihood could reflect typicality instead, and vice versa. Both ratings are therefore needed. Neither rating is a measure of lexical entailment or of children's own knowledge: both are adult reference measures of the selected V1–object–result relations.

## 1.3 Children's Comprehension of RVCs and V1s

Comprehension studies have examined how children interpret RVCs and V1s by presenting events with or without the result and asking children to judge perfective descriptions. Mandarin-speaking children associate resultative predicates with completed events from around age three (Li & Bowerman, 1998) ⟦待核⟧. Chen (2017), testing children aged 2;6 to 6;1, ⟦待核：Chen 2017 关于 transparency 的结论，题目为 "When transparency doesn't mean ease"⟧. J. Liu et al. (2026) found that 3-year-olds sometimes accepted RVC sentences for events showing the action without the result, whereas 5-year-olds largely rejected them, and that 5-year-olds rejected monomorphemic causative sentences in such events more often than adults, with higher acceptance when *hǎojǐcì* "several times" was present ⟦待核⟧.

Children's rejection of V1 descriptions in zero-result events is open to several explanations, which make partly different predictions for the present design.

(a) *Result encoding.* Children may represent the V1, like the RVC, as encoding the result. On this account, V1 and RVC descriptions should be rejected to a similar degree in zero-result events, so that the effect of result realization should not differ between the two forms. A verb-specific version, in which only some V1s are represented as encoding the result, would instead predict that acceptance differs between expressions in an all-or-none way rather than varying gradually with the ratings.

(b) *Inference.* Children may represent the V1 as not encoding the result but find it harder than adults to set aside an inference that the result occurred. This account predicts that rejection in zero-result events increases with result likelihood and that likelihood matters less when the result is attained.

(c) *Pragmatic inadequacy.* Children may judge the V1 description true but inadequate. When an event shows an action that fails to produce its result, the result becomes relevant, and a description that leaves it unaddressed may be rejected as underinformative (Papafragou & Musolino, 2003; Skordos & Papafragou, 2016) ⟦待核⟧. Because a result is more relevant the more it is expected, this account also predicts more rejection with higher likelihood. A binary judgment cannot distinguish (b) from (c); children are also known to be more tolerant of underinformative descriptions in binary than in graded tasks (Katsos & Bishop, 2011) ⟦待核⟧.

(d) *Atypical instance.* Children may reject the V1 description because a failed attempt is an atypical instance of the action (Section 1.2). This account predicts that rejection varies with action typicality.

The comparison of RVC and V1 descriptions bears on (a): a smaller effect of result realization on V1 than on RVC descriptions would indicate that children do not treat the V1 as encoding the result in the way the RVC does. The rating analyses bear on (b)–(d). Accounts (b) and (c) predict an association with likelihood, and account (d) predicts an association with typicality. Because the two ratings are correlated in the present materials, the study asks whether either rating is associated with children's judgments on its own and whether either explains additional variation after the other is taken into account. If neither explains variation beyond the other, the results will leave open whether children's judgments track likelihood, typicality, or information shared by both.

## 1.4 Children's Production of RVCs and V1s

Comprehension shows how children evaluate descriptions supplied to them; production shows which information they choose to express when no description is supplied. Children produce RVCs productively from early on (Chen, 2008; Deng, 2019) and also produce compound forms that indicate knowledge of their internal structure (Hsu et al., 2019) ⟦待核⟧. At the same time, young children often use monomorphemic verbs where adults would use compounds (Chen, 2008; Deng, 2019) ⟦待核⟧, so lower RVC use need not mean that a result goes unexpressed. Production research has largely asked whether and when children form RVCs; it has rarely compared descriptions of matched events in which the same action does or does not attain its result.

This comparison is informative because RVC use and mention of the result do not coincide. When the result is attained, a speaker may describe the event with an RVC, name only the action, name only the result (e.g., *miè le* "went out"), or express the action and the result in separate predicates. When the result is not attained, a speaker may describe only the action or mention the result in order to deny it, as in *méi bāo-kāi* "did not peel open." A child who produces fewer RVCs may therefore leave the result unmentioned or express it in another form. Distinguishing these possibilities requires coding both whether an RVC is used and, among responses without an RVC, whether the action, the target result, or both are expressed, as well as whether a mentioned result is affirmed or negated. Responses that affirm the target result in a zero-result event would indicate that the speaker described an unattained result as attained.

## 1.5 The Present Study

The study comprises a comprehension experiment and an elicited-production experiment with independent samples of 4-year-olds, 5-year-olds, and adults. Both use the same 40 videos: ten pairs of actions, each pair sharing an object and a target result, with each action filmed in a full-result and a zero-result version. In each pair, one V1–object expression was selected as expected to be more strongly associated with the target result than the other (e.g., *bāo* "peel" vs. *niē* "squeeze" a lychee, with the target result *kāi* "open"), so that the 20 expressions form two sets of ten. Adult ratings of result likelihood and action typicality characterize how the members of each pair differ and how large that difference is.

**Experiment 1** tests children's interpretation of RVC and V1 descriptions using a yes–no judgment task, in which acceptance serves as the measure of interpretation. It asks two questions.

*RQ1.* Do children's interpretations of V1 and RVC descriptions differ in their sensitivity to result realization, as indexed by whether acceptance of the two forms differs between full-result and zero-result events?

*RQ2.* Within pairs matched for object and target result, does children's acceptance of V1 descriptions vary with adults' continuous ratings of (a) result likelihood and (b) action typicality? Because the two ratings are correlated, each is tested separately, and the study then asks whether either explains additional variation after the other is taken into account, rather than assuming in advance that children's judgments reflect likelihood alone.

RVC descriptions are expected to be accepted for full-result and rejected for zero-result events, although younger children may accept some RVC descriptions of zero-result events (J. Liu et al., 2026). V1 descriptions are expected to be accepted less often in zero-result than in full-result events, but this difference should be smaller than for RVCs if children do not treat V1 as encoding the result (against account a). If children's judgments track result likelihood (accounts b and c), acceptance of V1 descriptions in zero-result events should decrease as within-pair likelihood increases, and this association should be weaker in full-result events. If they track typicality (account d), the corresponding pattern should hold for typicality. No directional prediction is made about which rating, if either, explains variation beyond the other.

**Experiment 2** examines how children describe the same events in an open-ended video-description task. It first asks whether realization of the target result affects whether children include an RVC in their descriptions. Within responses that contain only monomorphemic verbs and do not mention other results, it then asks whether children express the action only, the target result only, or both in separate predicates. Whether a mentioned target result is affirmed or negated is coded separately. Full-result events are expected to elicit more RVCs than zero-result events, because an absent result is expressed only if the speaker chooses to deny it. The likelihood and typicality ratings are not used as primary predictors in Experiment 2: they were collected for specified V1–object expressions, whereas the production task supplies no expression and the videos were not rated on these dimensions.

Because the two experiments use independent samples and different response demands, they provide complementary evidence about how children interpret and express actions and results, but they do not test a within-child relation between comprehension and production, and comparisons across tasks remain descriptive.
